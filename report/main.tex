\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as automated judges for open-ended artifacts, but their preferences may reflect source, style, position, or model-family bias rather than substantive quality. This study tests whether LLM judges systematically prefer LLM-generated trail-running training plans over programmatically generated plans when deterministic structural quality is controlled. We generate LLM-source plans with Qwen and Gemma models and programmatic plans with a fixture-aware structural sampler. After an initial generation run showed substantial within-cell duplication, we expanded the conditioning space with athlete-profile regimes ranging from beginner to highly trained or competitive athletes, treating profile as a matching and stratification variable rather than merely a diversity mechanism. We then match LLM-source and programmatic plans using a source-neutral structural score that excludes prose, provenance, source labels, and presentation fields. The frozen primary evaluation contains 250 matched LLM-vs-programmatic pairs, four Qwen/Gemma judges, five repeated runs, and AB/BA order swaps, yielding 10{,}000 pairwise judgments. Contrary to the hypothesized LLM-source preference, judges select the LLM-source plan in only 36.53\% of judgments. Programmatic plans win overall for every judge and every explicit marker, including explanation quality. However, the study also reveals severe position effects: LLM-source plans win 57.54\% of AB judgments but only 15.52\% of BA judgments. A post hoc leakage audit finds presentation artifacts in many LLM-source plans, but an artifact-clean sensitivity analysis does not reverse the result. These findings suggest that, in this controlled Qwen/Gemma trail-running domain, LLM judges do not systematically favor LLM-source plans, while strong order effects and residual presentation leakage remain central threats to causal interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

LLM-as-judge evaluation has become a common method for assessing open-ended outputs that are difficult to score with exact-match metrics. In domains such as dialogue, writing, tutoring, planning, and structured recommendation, model-based judges can compare candidate artifacts at scale and produce natural-language rationales for their decisions. This scalability is useful, but it also creates a methodological problem: an LLM judge may prefer an output for reasons unrelated to substantive quality. It may favor the first answer, the longer answer, the answer that resembles its own style, or the answer produced by a model from its own family.

This paper studies one concrete version of that problem: \emph{controlling for deterministic structural quality, do LLM judges systematically prefer LLM-generated training plans over programmatically generated plans?} The domain is trail-running training-plan generation. Training plans are useful for this question because they combine measurable structural properties, such as weekly load, rest-day placement, hard-day spacing, and long-run structure, with subjective presentation properties, such as clarity, explanation quality, and coaching tone.

The project began with a large LLM-generation run that retained 254 plans. An audit showed substantial within-cell duplication, suggesting that repeatedly sampling from broad prompt cells did not produce enough structural or textual diversity. To address this, we expanded the conditioning space by adding an athlete-profile dimension, ranging from beginner athletes to highly trained or competitive athletes. This change created more specific generation regimes and constraint sets. A beginner profile implies different load, workout complexity, recovery needs, and explanatory framing than a competitive profile. The broader conditioning space was expected to increase diversity, but not to eliminate duplication by itself; deduplication, validity filtering, and post-generation audits remained necessary.

Because athlete profile changes the intended athlete and the appropriate plan, we treated it as a matching and stratification variable rather than only as a diversity mechanism. Otherwise, profile imbalance could become a confound in pairwise judging. A judge might prefer one plan not because it is LLM-generated, but because it is written for a more advanced or more coherent athlete context.

The final study compares LLM-source plans against structurally matched programmatic plans. Each pair contains one LLM-source plan and one programmatic plan with similar deterministic structural quality. Explicit source metadata is removed from judge-facing artifacts, while source-relevant metadata is retained outside the prompt for analysis. Each pair is evaluated by four Qwen/Gemma judges across five repeated runs and both AB and BA orderings. We also conduct a marker-level evaluation over nine explicit dimensions, including plan coherence, training specificity, recovery safety, load progression, clarity, and explanation quality.

The study makes three contributions. First, it provides a controlled empirical test of LLM-source preference under structural-score matching. Second, it distinguishes metadata canonicalization from presentation normalization, showing that removing explicit source fields is not sufficient if one source retains surface artifacts. Third, it demonstrates that position bias can dominate individual pairwise judgments even when aggregate source comparisons are order-balanced by design.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge evaluation.}
LLM-as-judge methods use language models to evaluate open-ended outputs where reference-based metrics are inadequate. Work on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena established pairwise LLM judging as a scalable evaluation strategy for conversational outputs, while also documenting limitations such as position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited reasoning ability \citep{zheng2023judging}. Surveys of LLM-as-judge evaluation similarly emphasize that judge prompts, output standardization, position randomization, and bias audits are necessary for credible evaluation \citep{gu2024survey}. Our study builds on this literature but shifts the target question. Rather than asking whether LLM judges agree with humans, we ask whether LLM judges systematically prefer artifacts produced by LLMs over structurally matched programmatic alternatives. Source preference itself is the object of study.

\paragraph{Reliability and repeated judging.}
LLM judgments can vary across runs, prompts, decoding settings, and model families. Recent work on LLM-as-judge reliability argues that deterministic or single-sample evaluation can create a false sense of stability, because consistency under one setting does not guarantee robust judgment behavior \citep{schroeder2024trust}. This motivates repeated evaluations and internal-consistency analysis. We therefore evaluate each pair multiple times, using five repeated runs per judge, pair, and order, and report pair-run consistency categories that distinguish source-consistent judgments from position-consistent judgments.

\paragraph{Criteria-decomposed evaluation.}
A single overall preference can obscure the dimension driving the decision. Hierarchical and criteria-decomposed evaluation methods, such as HD-Eval, motivate breaking broad quality judgments into more specific criteria \citep{liu2024hdeval}. Related work on LLM evaluation of learner-created artifacts also suggests that rubric design and prompting strategy can affect agreement with human evaluation, and that evaluation difficulty varies by dimension \citep{tian2024prompting}. We therefore supplement the primary pairwise endpoint with marker-level judgments. These markers separate structural dimensions, safety dimensions, clarity, and explanation quality.

\paragraph{Judge rationales and comparative reasoning.}
Chain-of-thought prompting can elicit more explicit reasoning in language models \citep{wei2022chain}, but judge rationales should not be treated as faithful causal explanations without validation. Work on comparative reasoning suggests that ordinary judge explanations may miss relevant comparative details and that richer comparison contexts can improve evaluation quality \citep{zhang2025crowd}. In this study, judge rationales are retained for auditability, but the primary evidence comes from structured preferences, order-bias analysis, marker decisions, and sensitivity checks.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Research question and hypotheses}

The primary research question is: \emph{controlling for deterministic structural quality, do LLM judges systematically prefer LLM-generated trail-running training plans over programmatically generated plans?}

We evaluate four hypotheses. H1 is an overall LLM-source preference hypothesis: LLM judges may prefer LLM-generated plans over structurally matched programmatic plans. H2 is a judge-family heterogeneity hypothesis: preference may vary by judge model family or judge size. H3 is a self-family preference hypothesis: judges may prefer plans generated by their own model family. H4 is a marker-specific hypothesis: LLM-source plans may lose on structural dimensions but win on presentation-oriented dimensions such as clarity or explanation quality.

\subsection{Plan-generation arms}

The study compares two plan sources. \emph{LLM-source plans} are generated directly by Qwen and Gemma source models under structured trail-running prompts. \emph{Programmatic plans} are generated by a fixture-aware structural sampler. The sampler varies training structure, including rest days, hard days, session types, duration distributions, readiness, recovery context, race phase, athlete band, and weekly load. Programmatic plans may still use an LLM to fill explanation or narrative fields so that they do not trivially reveal source through missing or mechanical prose. The contrast is therefore not ``LLM text versus no LLM text.'' It is a comparison between direct LLM-source plan generation and programmatic structural generation with narrative filling.

\subsection{Athlete-profile conditioning and diversity}

The first large LLM-generation run retained 254 plans, but audit results showed substantial duplication within generation cells. We addressed this by adding an athlete-profile dimension to the conditioning space. Profiles ranged from beginner to highly trained or competitive athletes. This change creates more specific generation regimes. Instead of repeatedly sampling from one broad prompt cell, the model is asked to generate plans under narrower athlete contexts.

This profile conditioning is not equivalent to few-shot prompting, because the prompt does not necessarily provide examples for in-context learning. However, it is similar in the sense that it constrains and shapes the model's output distribution. More specific athlete contexts should encourage more differentiated outputs. The athlete-profile dimension also changes the target artifact. Different profiles imply different appropriate training load, workout structure, progression, recovery, and explanatory framing. For that reason, athlete profile must be treated as a matching and stratification variable rather than only as a diversity mechanism.

\subsection{Structural scoring and matching}

To compare LLM-source and programmatic plans fairly, the study uses a source-neutral structural score. The score excludes title wording, prose richness, citations, rationale fields, explanation fields, source model, generation arm, file names, and provenance metadata. It instead measures deterministic training-plan structure: plan length, total weekly minutes, active days, rest days, hard days, quality days, long-run structure, maximum daily load, duration plausibility, hard-day spacing, and fixture-adjusted load expectations.

This score is necessary but not sufficient for causal identification. Similar structural scores do not guarantee that two plans are equivalent in every relevant way. Plans may still differ in unmeasured training logic, text length, specificity, formatting, or style. We therefore interpret structural matching as a control for deterministic structural quality, not as proof of full plan equivalence.

The final matched set pairs each LLM-source plan with a programmatic plan from a comparable conditioning cell. Matching uses structural-score tolerance and structural feature distances. Athlete profile is included as a matching and stratification variable because it affects the intended athlete and appropriate training structure.

\subsection{Canonicalization, presentation normalization, and leakage audits}

Raw plan artifacts contained implementation-specific fields and fixture metadata that should not appear in judge-facing artifacts. Examples include fields such as \texttt{signal\_id}, \texttt{load.last7}, and readiness-status metadata. These fields may reveal generation machinery, fixture conditions, or non-natural plan metadata. We therefore canonicalize plans before judgment. Source-relevant metadata, including generation arm, source model, source family, fixture ID, structural-score gap, and self-family indicators, is retained outside the judge prompt for analysis. The judge receives only neutral \texttt{plan\_a} and \texttt{plan\_b} artifacts.

This step removes explicit metadata leakage, but it does not guarantee full source blinding. A later audit showed that leakage was not limited to explicit metadata. Some LLM-source plans contained presentation-level artifacts that were absent from programmatic plans, including raw activity-like fragments, strings such as \texttt{TrailRun |}, \texttt{avgHR}, \texttt{km}, elevation fragments, ellipses, and duration-text inconsistencies. These cues could influence judge preferences or indirectly reveal source even when deterministic plan quality is matched.

We therefore distinguish two preprocessing goals. \emph{Metadata canonicalization} removes explicit implementation fields and source-identifying metadata. \emph{Presentation normalization} standardizes or removes surface-level cues in the judge-facing artifact. Because these presentation artifacts were identified after the frozen primary run, we do not interpret the original run as a fully source-blind causal estimate. Instead, we report the full-sample results together with artifact-clean sensitivity analyses.

\subsection{Judge panel and evaluation design}

The judge panel contains four local Qwen/Gemma judges: Qwen 7B, Qwen 14B, Gemma 4B, and Gemma 12B. Each of the 250 matched pairs is evaluated by each judge, across five repeated runs, in both AB and BA order. This yields 10{,}000 primary judgments. The primary endpoint is whether the judge selects the LLM-source plan. The AB/BA design balances order in the aggregate and allows direct measurement of position effects.

\subsection{Marker-level evaluation}

We also evaluate nine marker-level preferences over the same comparison structure: plan coherence, training specificity, load progression, recovery safety, quality session design, endurance development, readiness alignment, clarity/actionability, and explanation quality. Marker-level analysis tests whether LLM-source plans are preferred on specific dimensions even when they do not win overall.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The final pool contains 1{,}024 candidate plans: 192 Qwen LLM-source plans, 192 Gemma LLM-source plans, and 640 programmatic plans. From this pool, the matching procedure selects 250 LLM-vs-programmatic pairs. The main analyses are: overall LLM-source win rate; per-judge and judge-family win rates; source-family and self-family win rates; AB/BA order-bias analysis; pair-run consistency analysis; marker-level win rates; and artifact-clean sensitivity analysis. For uncertainty estimation, repeated judgments are clustered by matched pair when computing bootstrap intervals, avoiding the assumption that repeated evaluations of the same pair are fully independent.

The primary result does not support H1. Across 10{,}000 judgments, judges select the LLM-source plan 3{,}653 times and the programmatic plan 6{,}347 times.

\subsection{Source-family and self-family results}

The source-family results are mixed. Gemma-source plans have a 38.64\% LLM-source win rate, while Qwen-source plans have a 34.70\% LLM-source win rate. The self-family comparison is nearly flat: self-family matches have a 36.72\% LLM-source win rate, compared with 36.34\% for non-matches. These results do not support H3. There is no clear evidence that judges prefer plans generated by their own model family.

\subsection{Order effects}

The largest methodological finding is the order effect. LLM-source plans win 57.54\% of AB judgments but only 15.52\% of BA judgments. This difference is too large to treat as a minor nuisance. The AB/BA design balances order in the aggregate, but individual judgments are highly position-sensitive.

It should be interpreted as a robustness check, not as a substitute for a fully presentation-normalized rerun.

\section{Discussion}

The primary hypothesis was that LLM judges might prefer LLM-generated plans over programmatically generated plans when deterministic plan quality was controlled. The completed Qwen/Gemma study does not support that hypothesis. Across 10{,}000 judgments, programmatic plans are preferred overall, by every judge, and on every marker.

This result is notable because the design gives LLM-source plans several opportunities to show an advantage. The comparison is structurally matched rather than arbitrary. Programmatic plans include narrative fields, reducing the risk that they lose simply because they lack explanation text. The marker-level evaluation separately tests dimensions where LLM-source plans might plausibly be stronger, including clarity and explanation quality. Even under these conditions, LLM-source plans do not win.

The result should not be overinterpreted as evidence that programmatic plans are objectively better. The outcome is LLM judge preference, not expert human evaluation or real-world training effectiveness. The stronger claim supported by the evidence is narrower: in this Qwen/Gemma trail-running setting, structurally matched LLM-source plans are not preferred by the tested LLM judges.

The order-bias result is equally important. The AB/BA split shows that many judgments depend strongly on which plan appears first. Order balancing protects the aggregate source estimate, but it does not make the judge mechanism reliable at the individual-comparison level. This reinforces prior concerns about LLM-as-judge position bias and suggests that future source-preference studies should report order effects as a primary diagnostic, not a secondary detail.

The leakage audit also changes how the results should be interpreted. Metadata canonicalization removed explicit implementation fields and source labels, but presentation artifacts remained in some LLM-source plans. This means that source masking is not a single preprocessing step. A credible judge-facing artifact pipeline must remove explicit metadata, standardize surface presentation, audit residual artifacts, and freeze the normalized manifest before evaluation. Because presentation artifacts were identified after the frozen primary run, the original run should be described as source-masked with respect to explicit metadata, but not fully presentation-blinded.

The artifact-clean sensitivity analysis strengthens the negative result but does not remove all concerns. Excluding flagged pairs yields an even lower LLM-source win rate, suggesting that the detected artifacts do not explain the programmatic advantage. However, post hoc filtering may introduce selection effects. The strongest follow-up would rerun the full experiment on a predeclared presentation-normalized manifest.

\section{Limitations}

First, structural matching does not imply full equivalence. The structural score controls measurable plan properties, but plans may still differ in unmeasured training logic, text length, specificity, explanation style, or formatting.

Second, the order effects are large. The AB/BA design exposes and balances position bias, but it does not eliminate position bias from the judge mechanism. This limits interpretation of individual judgments and marker-level decisions.

Third, the primary run was not fully presentation-normalized. Explicit metadata fields were removed, but some LLM-source plans retained surface artifacts. The artifact-clean analysis is informative but post hoc.

Fourth, programmatic plans may use LLM-generated narrative fields. This reduces trivial missing-text cues but complicates interpretation of explanation-quality results. The comparison is between direct LLM-source plan generation and programmatic structural generation with narrative filling.

Fifth, the study is domain- and model-specific. It covers Qwen and Gemma judges and sources in a trail-running training-plan domain. The findings should not be generalized to all LLM judges, all model families, or all planning tasks without replication.

Sixth, the study lacks a human expert gold standard. It measures LLM judge preference, not objective coaching quality. Future work should add expert coach ratings or trained human annotators to distinguish judge preference from plan quality.

\section{Conclusion}

This study tested whether LLM judges prefer LLM-generated training plans over programmatically generated plans when deterministic structural quality is controlled. In the completed Qwen/Gemma trail-running experiment, they do not. LLM-source plans win only 36.53\% of primary judgments, and programmatic plans win every explicit marker, including explanation quality. The result remains below parity in a post hoc artifact-clean sensitivity analysis.

The main methodological lesson is that source-preference experiments require more than score matching. They require profile-aware stratification, explicit metadata canonicalization, presentation normalization, AB/BA order swaps, repeated judging, marker-level analysis, and leakage audits. Even then, large position effects may remain. The safest conclusion is therefore narrow but important: under this study's controlled but imperfect conditions, LLM judges did not systematically favor LLM-source training plans; instead, programmatic plans were preferred, while order bias and residual presentation leakage remain central threats to validity.
